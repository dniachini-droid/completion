# Story pitches

> Phase 3, stage 2 of the overnight run (D-015). **Open document: Dan reads this.** Nothing here gives away any story's answers; each pitch is written at the level of the back of the box and the first hour of play.
> All three fit the agreed game (`game/GAME_DESIGN.md`, D-010–D-013): a sealed place explored by real action; a script learned sign by sign whose words are powers; records that are the linked lives of people from different ages; Keys and words; the first word in week 2–3. They differ in *what the place is, what the script is, who the antagonist is, and what kind of ending is latent in the premise*, not only in skin.

_Written 2026-09-23. Chosen pitch: **1, The Long Answer** (D-016, confirmed by Dan 2026-09-24, D-024)._

## Pitch 1 — The Long Answer

**Back of the box.** Somewhere under the world there is a quiet place that was not made by us. Its walls are cut with a script that does things when it is read. People have found it before: a salt-cutter after an earthquake, a surveyor sent to run a tunnel, a woman who read about the place in a railway engineer's log and came to see. Each of them learned a few marks. Each of them met the same tall figure, who has been there in every age, under a different name. Long ago the makers heard something, and began an answer that would take longer than any age to finish. You are the latest to come down. The lamp is already lit.

**Fantasy, tone, setting.** *"Every real hour takes me further down, and what I find was left for whoever came next."* The setting is our own world, but no age is ever named or dated: the records speak in bronze sickles, measuring cords, blasting powder, field notebooks, and Dan works out the age from the objects. Under the surface is the Quiet (working name): a deep, dry, cut-stone place with lamps that wake when spoken to, doors that answer words, and a long descent whose bottom no one in the records reached. The tone is cosmic and dark in the *Three-Body* and *Eternal Darkness* register, vast timescales, something listening, a civilisation that ended, but the dread lives in specific rooms and specific records, and the rest is grandeur and melancholy. The people in the records are ordinary, concrete and often funny, and all of them are finished: nothing waits on Dan.

**The script and the powers.** The marks are cut into the stone; the app's working name for the script is *the Cut*. It is not an alphabet: each mark is a concept, and marks share parts (the mark for *lamp* contains the mark for *fire*). Words are two to four marks cut together, and a word is a power because the place obeys it: light, open, wake, hold, and later stranger things. Forming a word is a short fixed ritual, like a dialling sequence: mark, mark, the word locks, the door answers. Names are ringed, so Dan recognises a recurring name before he can read a single mark. Function marks (not, once, all) arrive later and change the meaning of old records at a stroke.

**What the linked lives feel like.** Five to seven people across the ages, each with their own form of record (a tally-song, a report to a superior, a copyist's margins, a blasting log, field notes), each with a different name for the place and for the tall figure, and each holding a piece the others lacked. Two lives touch by month four; a later life visibly misreads an earlier one; the last life learned enough to write *in the script itself*. One recurring family of objects (a clay lamp, a measuring cord, a copied book, a cutting rod) passes from hand to hand and ends in Dan's.

**Why it fits Dan.** It is the pitch in which the ending is *latent in the premise* (a place built to finish an answer must end with the answer), which is what "goes somewhere" needs. The figure is present from week one in every age, imposing and mysterious, and the question of what he wants drives the middle of the story; his reasons, when they come, are ones a sane person could hold. Language-as-power (*Arrival*, Stargate), linked lives in one place (*Eternal Darkness*), an alien intelligence at a vast distance (*Three-Body*) and a hub whose gates need what you have earned (*Ocarina*) are all structural here, not decorative.

**Main risk.** The cosmic-message premise sits near *Three-Body* and *Arrival*; it must be won on specifics (the people, the objects, the exact nature of the answer), and the sealed design avoids their particular twists. The ending is quiet by nature rather than a conquest; it must be made active and emotionally large or it will feel small.

## Pitch 2 — The Unmade

**Back of the box.** A mountain was hollowed out by a people who caught something that fell from the sky and could not kill it. So they wrote it down: they took it apart into marks and cut the marks into the mountain, and the thing became a language. For three thousand years, anyone who learned a word could do what that word was: a shepherd became a king, a nun became a plague, a foreman became a storm. Every word taken out of the mountain is a word the mountain no longer holds. You have found the door. The voice inside is very reasonable.

**Fantasy, tone, setting.** *"I am learning to speak a god, one word at a time, and it is learning me."* Dark mythic fantasy on a real-feeling but unnamed world: a mountain kingdom, a monastery built into the flank, a mine, a modern survey station; each age reached the mountain by a different road. The tone is *Eternal Darkness* and *Annihilation*: intimate horror, beautiful and wrong, with a voice that talks to the reader through whoever holds the most words. Dread is constant but never cruel to Dan himself.

**The script and the powers.** The script is the thing's anatomy. Each mark is a part of it (a limb, a sense, an appetite), and words are the acts it could perform. Powers are raw: to make light is to borrow its eye. The grammar is *alignment first*, as in the *Eternal Darkness* runes: every word carries a mark saying which part of the thing it belongs to, and that mark is the lore.

**What the linked lives feel like.** The takers and the ones who chased them: each life is the story of one word leaving the mountain and what it did in the world. Voices are vivid and unreliable; several are villains in their own age. They converge because the words they took are the words Dan is learning, in the same order.

**Why it fits Dan.** The most direct hit on "runes that combine into spells" and on "the darker the better". The antagonist speaks early and often and has real charisma. Composable power is the whole game.

**Main risk.** "Sealed evil in a can" is the oldest well in the genre, and here Dan's own progress (taking words out) could read as damage to the world, which cuts against principle 8 and against the game's purpose. The lives are episodic (one word each) rather than one converging story, so "everything was related" is weaker. The ending options are release, refusal or reassembly, all familiar.

## Pitch 3 — The Last Run

**Back of the box.** The world has ended before. Under it there is an engine, older than the crust, and every few thousand years it starts again, and a new age grows on top of it like moss, and falls. The engine keeps a copy of a few minds from every age. You are reading them. Their language is the engine's own: a control script, where a word is an instruction. Somewhere in the engine is the one who decides when the next run begins, and it has decided that this age, yours, is nearly over. It is not angry. It is very patient. It is waiting for a better result.

**Fantasy, tone, setting.** *"I am learning to give orders to the machine that ends the world."* Hard science-fantasy: a dead engine city, cold and vast, *Mass Effect*'s Reapers by way of *Blindsight* and *Horizon*. The ages are strange (the records are from civilisations that were not ours: a people who counted in tides, a people who lived in the walls), which is the most alien and most "other civilisations" of the three. Dread is total and clean; warmth comes from the kept minds, who are funny and grieving and argue with one another across runs.

**The script and the powers.** A control language: verbs (start, halt, keep, copy, seal) and objects (lamp, door, lift, memory). Words are literally commands, and the engine obeys. The most powerful words are the ones the Operator uses. Learning the script is learning to operate the world.

**What the linked lives feel like.** One or two minds per run, across five or six runs, each a different civilisation. They converge because each mind found the same fault in the engine and left a note about it for the next run.

**Why it fits Dan.** Alien civilisations, futuristic powers that look like magic, an imposing non-human antagonist with a reason it can state calmly, and a thematic centre (no more clean slates; the world must go on with what it has) that is strong and unusual.

**Main risk.** The cycle-of-civilisations structure is *Mass Effect*'s and *Horizon*'s, and Dan will see it. The lives are the most alien and hardest to hold at one fragment a day; the setting is the coldest, and there is little room for a human-scale accomplice with a comprehensible grief. The ending ("end the runs") is a single switch, so it is hard to make it a re-reading of everything before.

---

## The choice: Pitch 1, The Long Answer

**Reasons.**
1. **It goes somewhere by construction.** The premise (a place built to finish an answer over ages) makes the ending the only possible ending and still leaves the *how* to be discovered. Pitch 2's endings are all familiar; Pitch 3's ending is a switch.
2. **Everything can be related.** The lives are not episodes but pieces: each holds part of what the last life needs, one family of objects passes through every hand, and the place itself is the spine. That is the "everything was related" structure Dan named as his favourite thing about *Ocarina* and *Eternal Darkness*.
3. **The figure is the strongest.** A figure present in every age from week one, imposing, whose intentions are the story's central question: his depth can be revealed by the staircase the research recommends (effect, presence, face, reason, nature), and his reason is one a sane person could hold. Pitch 2's voice is charismatic but not mysterious; Pitch 3's Operator is a system.
4. **It fits the kind app.** All its writers are finished; nothing waits; progress is excavation. Pitch 2 risks making Dan's progress feel like damage.
5. **It is the least like any one of Dan's favourites while touching all of them**, so it can be unique on its specifics rather than a tribute.
