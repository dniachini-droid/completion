# The voice of the story's words

> **Spoiler-free.** How the game's story text should sound, chosen by Dan (D-086) from six published passages: he loved **Le Guin** (*The Tombs of Atuan*), **VanderMeer** (*Annihilation*) and **Macfarlane** (*Underland*), and disliked **McCarthy** (*The Road*). Every writer of the story's lines, human or agent, works to this page.

## The blend in one line

**Le Guin's plain, weighty words, VanderMeer's cool exactness, and Macfarlane's rock-in-the-hand wonder.** Said quietly, and never preached.

## What each brings

- **Le Guin (B): plain words, mythic weight.** Short common words: stone, dark, lamp, door, salt, silence. Rhythm does the work: lists that gather ("and bones, and years, and silence"), a short sentence that closes a long one. The place feels old because the words are simple and sure, not because they say "ancient".
- **VanderMeer (E): cool exactness; strangeness stated flat.** When something is impossible, say it plainly and move on: *"The tower, which was not supposed to be there…"* No exclamation, no "somehow", no "eerie". The calm is what unsettles.
- **Macfarlane (F): real rock, the body, deep time.** Real textures and physics: grain, fracture, cold that comes up through the soles, air that moves, the weight of the hill overhead. The explorer's body is present: knees, breath, fingertips, the lamp's warmth on a knuckle. Wonder comes from knowing how long stone takes.

## What we never do (McCarthy's side, C, and the AI habits)

- No heavy, grand rhetoric. No biblical cadence, no "like pilgrims in a fable". At most one simile per passage, and a small, physical one ("like a banister", "like frost in a crack").
- No telling the reader what to feel: never *eerie, haunting, mysterious, breathtaking, awe-inspiring*.
- No AI tells: *a testament to, whispers of, echoes of, ancient secrets, tapestry, palpable, beckons, a sense of, journey, embark, delve into, as if time itself*; no triplets of adjectives; no stock sentences reused from line to line.
- No surveying: not "you can see X beside Y", not "it occupies". Put the reader there instead of describing the layout.

## Sentences

- Mostly short and medium; now and then one long sentence that gathers, then a short one that lands.
- Concrete nouns and strong plain verbs. Few adjectives; one exact one beats three vague ones.
- Present tense, "you". British English.
- One thing looked at properly is better than five things listed.
- End a line on something the reader will still see afterwards: an object, a sound, a light. Never on a moral.

## A made-up example (not from the story)

*Not this:* "You can see the ancient, mysterious steps occupying the far wall, a testament to the hands that shaped them."

*This:* "The steps go down into the dark. Each one is worn to a shallow dish in the middle, and cold comes up out of them like water."

## How it is used

A writer works **one place at a time**, as one continuous piece, then cuts it into the screens; lines are never written one by one in isolation. Critics check each place against this page, against the story's facts, and for AI tells. Dan judges results blind.
