# Narrative research — what makes long mysteries work

> Phase 3, stage 1 of the overnight run (D-008, D-015). **Open document: spoiler-free for this game.** Spoilers for *other* works are marked **[spoiler: title]**; skip those lines if you haven't finished the work.
> Four parallel research passes (mystery craft; decipherment games; Dan's favourite works; fragment and archive storytelling) were synthesised into this file. The lessons at the end are the binding output; everything else is the evidence.

_Written 2026-09-23._

## The problem this research serves

The story will be delivered in an unusual form: about 250 arrivals a year, roughly half carrying a record fragment readable in well under a minute, plus up to ~250 sealed things opened by Keys. That is a story told in ~125 postcards over twelve months, with a mechanic (learning signs) that forces old fragments to be re-read. Almost all craft writing assumes a reader who holds a whole book in mind for hours. So each section below ends with what transfers to this format.

## Part 1 — Why long mysteries work, and why some go nowhere

### Promise, progress, payoff
Brandon Sanderson's framing: a story makes a **promise** (what kind of story, what question it will answer), shows legible **progress**, and delivers a **payoff** that is *surprising in the how, not the what*. Two consequences for us:
- **Progress must be instrumented.** Readers forgive slow stories, not stories where they can't tell whether they are moving. A mystery's progress is *how precisely the question can now be stated*. In this game progress is visible by construction (signs known, records readable, lives linked, depth reached in the Site), but only if each fragment actually moves one of those.
- **The promise must be made early and restated.** A promise made in the first three fragments is forgotten by month four unless it recurs. Dan's own preference ("I know roughly what's coming; the details surprise") is exactly Sanderson's payoff rule.

### Fair play: clues, hints, and "it was there the whole time"
Knox's Decalogue (1929), Van Dine's rules and Ellery Queen's "challenge to the reader" are one principle: **the reader must have had everything the detective had.** The modern equivalent is the re-watch audit: *The Sixth Sense*, *Arrival* and *Dark* survive a second viewing because the truth was continuously present and the misdirection was the viewer's *assumption*, not the author's *lie*. Rule of thumb: **you may lie through a character; you may not lie through the camera.** For us: a record's writer may be wrong, biased or lying; the Site, its physical evidence and the script's mechanics never lie.

- A **clue** constrains the answer and is recoverable afterwards as having pointed at it. A **hint** is tonal steering that proves nothing. Clues make a reveal honest; hints make it felt. Works that land use many hints, a few load-bearing clues, and at least one clue placed early and conspicuously enough that the reader remembers *choosing to discount it*.
- "It was there the whole time" needs three things: the clue was shown doing another job in the scene; the reader was handed a **sufficient wrong explanation**; and at the reveal the story **points back** to the earlier instance. Our re-reading mechanic gives the third almost free. The risk is skipping the first two because the third is so convenient.

### Double-meaning clues and re-interpretation
The engine of every satisfying twist is a **fixed fact whose meaning changes with context**: who wrote it, when, to whom, what a word means, what the writer knew. The reader gets no new information, only old information re-sorted, which is why it feels earned. Christie's literally-true sentences the reader parses figuratively; Gene Wolfe's narrator who "remembers everything" and is quietly contradicted by his own text; **[spoiler: Dark]** a slogan ("the beginning is the end") that turns out to be a literal description of the plot.

This game has a *literal* version: a fragment read with 40% of its signs known yields one honest but incomplete sentence; at 80% it yields a different one. That is honest only if the partial reading is a genuine sub-reading of the full text, **true but misleading by omission, never false**. Practically: write every fragment in full, then record what is legible at each sign-coverage band, and check each partial reading.

### Planned versus improvised endings: what actually happened
| Work | Planned? | Outcome | Lesson |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Babylon 5* | Five-year outline written first; a "trap door" for every character | Prophecies paid off on schedule; season-1 lines read as planted | Plan the *shape of the answers*, not the cast |
| *Lost* | Questions posed without answers ("mystery box"); end date fixed late | **[spoiler: Lost]** prominent hooks (the numbers, Walt) never answered in proportion to their prominence | A question's prominence writes a cheque |
| *Battlestar Galactica* (2004) | "They have a plan" written because it sounded good; no plan | Lands for many because the *thematic* promise was kept where the *plot* promise was improvised | A fixed thematic ending can carry improvised plot, but viewers notice |
| *Twin Peaks* | Never meant to answer its central question; forced to | Lost its engine mid-season 2 | The central question must be one you are willing to answer |
| *The X-Files* mytharc | Layers added outward each season | Never resolved | An escalating question ladder must be bounded in advance |
| *Dark* | Ending fixed; final season written with the end known | Every reveal recontextualises a season-1 scene | Decide the family tree before the first episode |
| *Breaking Bad* | Direction and ending tone fixed; details improvised | Worked because its mysteries are "what will he do", not "what is true" | Improvise "what will they do"; plan "what is true". Ours is the second kind |
| *Fullmetal Alchemist* (manga) | Ending and central truth fixed before serialisation | Nearly every named character is load-bearing; early background fires in the finale | Satisfaction of "all connected" is proportional to how *ordinary* the details looked at first |
| *Attack on Titan* | Ending decided from the start | Dense foreshadowing; contested last chapters | Budget the last 10% as generously as the first 10% |
| *Westworld* | Season 1 a pre-solved puzzle; later seasons puzzle-style without a solved puzzle | Trust decayed season by season | Puzzle structure without a pre-solved puzzle is a promise you can't keep |
| *The Leftovers* | Declared its central event unanswerable in episode one | Widely loved ending; character mysteries answered | You may refuse a question only up front and loudly, and never the one progress is measured by |
| *Six Feet Under* | Premise makes the ending latent | "Inevitable and surprising at once" | The best endings are latent in the premise |

**Pattern.** The planned works wrote the *truth* before the *telling* and designed the telling as a controlled leak. The improvised ones that landed fixed the thematic destination. The ones that went nowhere **asked questions priced by how intriguing they were, not by whether they could be answered.**

Also relevant: *Higurashi* and *Umineko* (mystery through *variants of one situation*; the invariant across variants is the clue) and Wolfe's *Book of the New Sun* (first reading an adventure, second reading a different book, because the narrator *reports* and never *explains*). That is the found-record posture: writers report in their own vocabulary; only the player's growing knowledge turns reports into explanation.

### Structural patterns for a long mystery told through records
- **The onion**: each peeled layer reframes the one above. Works with a finite, pre-known core; fails when layers are added outward.
- **The hourglass / funnel**: timelines converging on one point; with several ages it becomes *many lives, one place, one moment*.
- **The spiral**: return to the same events at widening scale, each pass adding a dimension. Re-reading *is* a spiral.
- **The unreliable archive**: *Dracula* (dated documents collated by the reader; no narrator sees the whole), *Pale Fire* (the commentator is the mystery), *House of Leaves* (nested editors). Two lessons: documents need an **eventually decodable age-marker** so collation is possible; and **if someone curated the archive, they are a suspect.**

These are not alternatives: the archive is the container, the funnel the plot shape, the onion the reveal schedule, the spiral the re-reading experience. The funnel is the one the player should be able to *name* by month three.

### Pacing revelations over a year
Serialised mystery settles at roughly one model-changing reveal every 6–10 episodes. For ~125 fragments over 52 weeks: **8–10 model-changing reveals a year (one every 5–7 weeks), a confirming reveal every 1–2 weeks, and something noticeable in nearly every fragment.** A fragment that adds nothing teaches the player that visits are optional.

- **The question ladder** must be counted and capped: each answer opens a question *larger in scope but smaller in number*. The failure mode is an unbounded ladder where each answer opens *more* questions rather than a *bigger* one.
- **The mid-point** (month 5–7) should change the *category* of the mystery, ideally through a single sign or word that makes a large fraction of already-read fragments say something different.
- **Order answers by scope, smallest first**, not by how exciting they are to write. Let a good suspicion sit three or four fragments before confirming it, so the player owns it.

### Antagonists with depth
The antagonists that worked follow one staircase: **effect → presence → face → reason → nature.**
- Ganondorf: presence and face early; reason and nature came games later. **[spoiler: Wind Waker]** the "wind" speech (a desert people's envy of Hyrule's green wind) is the reason, delivered years after the menace.
- Vader: face withheld, effect immediate; the reason arrives at the mid-point and re-reads earlier scenes without contradicting them.
- The Reapers: effect (dead civilisations) → rumour → one ship's voice → reason last. **[spoiler: Mass Effect 3]** the reason was introduced and resolved in one unplanted conversation, which is where the staircase collapsed. **The top rung needs the most planting, not the least.**
- Pious Augustus (*Eternal Darkness*): the player *plays* the antagonist's origin in the prologue, then sees his hand from every later age.
- Ye Wenjie (*Three-Body*): revealed through her own sympathetically told past, so her decision is understood before it is judged. **A record in the antagonist's own hand is the strongest depth tool a found-document story has.**

A **reason** makes an antagonist coherent; being **"right"** makes him a rival thesis. The strongest form for dark science-fantasy: **right about the problem, wrong about the cure.** The player agrees with the diagnosis (which is why the antagonist frightens) and rejects the remedy (which is why the ending is a victory, not a tragedy). This also supports a fixed ending: the ending becomes the protagonist's alternative answer to a question the antagonist posed correctly.

## Part 2 — Games built on decipherment and knowledge

### Heaven's Vault (Inkle, 2019)
46 base glyphs compound into words ("person" + "one" = *I*; add possession and it is *my*). The language was designed **from the dictionary outward**: the plot's inscriptions were written first, then the glyph set engineered so key words share parts the player can reason about. For each unknown word the game offers four candidate meanings; the guess is recorded with a question mark and **confirmed or rejected later**, once enough corroborating inscriptions have been seen. Wrong guesses propagate into every later inscription until rejected, then everything re-renders: built-in re-translation. What worked: compounding gives real "aha" moments; deferred confirmation feels like scholarship, not a quiz; wrong guesses become story rather than failure. What frustrated: early inscriptions with no context (blind guessing), near-synonym candidates, and words that never closed.

### Chants of Sennaar (Rundisc, 2023)
Five peoples, five scripts of ~30–40 glyphs, one per layer of a tower; each people's way of life was designed first and the language followed. Meaning is inferred from staging (a figure points at a door and says two glyphs; a lever labelled with one glyph opens a gate). Guesses re-render every line immediately, so wrong guesses produce visibly wrong sentences. Validation is by **page**: several glyphs are matched to small pictures at once and the page only resolves when all are right, so guessing is inefficient. Each language has one or two distinctive grammar rules (reduplicated plurals, bracketed negation, unusual word order), enough to make peoples feel different.

### Outer Wilds (Mobius Digital, 2019)
Knowledge is the only progression: no upgrades; every place reachable from minute one; what changes is what you know. The **rumour board** shows discoveries as nodes joined by lines with "there is more to explore here" markers, so there is always a next question without an assigned task. The ancient texts are **conversations** (spirals, one per speaker, branches as replies), informal, with recurring names, so a person met on one world turns up on another. **[spoiler: Outer Wilds]** Every thread converges on one fact and the ending needs four pieces of knowledge obtainable in any order; the "solution" is trivial once known. The game is entirely the knowing.

### Return of the Obra Dinn (Lucas Pope, 2018)
Fixed, complete evidence; a ledger the player fills; confirmation only **in sets of three** correct entries, so brute force fails but an educated set (two confident, one hunch) succeeds. It feels fair because the evidence never changes and each confirmed identity becomes a fixed point for the next inference.

### Tunic (2022)
A manual in an invented script that teaches everything from page one if you can read it; nothing unlocks mechanically from owning a page. **[spoiler: Tunic]** Late on it becomes clear the wall art and page borders were a second, directional script readable all along, by the *player* rather than the character.

### Shorter notes
*Fez* (one unsignposted in-world Rosetta; fine for a community, but a solo player needs the key findable on purpose). *Her Story* ("a word is a key": vocabulary is literally progress). *The Case of the Golden Idol* (fill-in-the-blank deductions validated in bulk; the last scene rewrites the first). *Animal Well* (layers of secrets; the player can stop at any layer feeling complete). *Sethian* (free composition of sentences in an alien script: elegant, but players stalled; too open for short sessions). *Lorelei and the Laser Eyes* (dozens of one-minute puzzles feeding one meta-mystery). *Riven* (a numeral system taught by one children's toy in a schoolroom). Zelda's Sheikah and Zonai scripts (decorative substitution ciphers, never required: proof that a consistent script on ruins has aesthetic value even with no decipherment play).

### Real-world decipherment as a design model
Champollion had a bilingual, the cartouche ring-fencing royal names, and names whose sounds were known (Ptolemy, Cleopatra); his leap was that the script was *mixed* (phonetic signs, logograms, silent determinatives). Old Persian cuneiform fell through predictable royal formulae ("X, great king, king of kings, son of Y"). Linear B fell without a bilingual: Kober's grid of signs sharing consonants or vowels, built *without assigning sounds*; then Ventris's guess that frequent Knossos-only words were place names. Maya fell when a mangled "alphabet" was re-read as a syllabary and tested against captioned pictures. Undeciphered scripts (Linear A, rongorongo, the Indus signs, the Voynich manuscript) resist because of too little text, no names, no bilingual, or not being language at all.

**Common ingredients:** proper names; a bilingual or picture-with-caption; sign count as diagnostic (~20–35 alphabetic, ~50–90 syllabic, hundreds logographic); recurring formulae; determinatives; structure before sound. A plausible invented script therefore wants: a logogram core plus a few function signs (plural, negation, possession, past) and determinatives; compositional glyphs (a "water" element recurring in "rain", "well", "sea"); two or three visible grammar rules; formulae met many times; names in cartouches recurring across records; and a count the player can hold, roughly 40–80 signs.

### Handed versus figured out
Games that *hand* signs (as loot) stop being play; games that make the player *figure them out* need concentrated sessions and can fail. The hybrid that fits one-minute visits: the **sign is earned in the world** (real-life effort), but its **meaning arrives with a context** (a fragment where it sits beside something known, a picture, a name); the player's act is a one-tap guess from three or four candidates, recorded provisionally, re-rendered in every record containing it, and confirmed later in small batches when another fragment corroborates. No free text, no timer, no lose state. He is never stuck: the next visit simply brings more context.

## Part 3 — What exactly makes Dan's favourite works hit

Dan's own vocabulary is the rubric: "everything was related"; "imposing" evil "with depth and story"; stories that "jumped between characters and were all linked"; runes that combine; language as power; ancient technology that looks like magic; the dialling sequence as "epic"; "knowing roughly what's coming, with the details a surprise".

**Eternal Darkness.** Linkage is *architectural*: the same corridors walked by different people centuries apart; what one hides in a wall another finds. The Tome is the frame object; the Roivas family is the spine, each generation inheriting unfinished work. The antagonist is the player's *first* character, so the enemy is known before any victim knows him; the Ancients are never fully shown and are learned through the *effect* of their alignment on the world. The rune grammar (circle + alignment + verb + noun) is the cosmology: the alignment rune *is* the lore, runes are found as artefacts dated to a century, and they are also *text*, so learning a rune half-reads an inscription seen chapters earlier. **[spoiler: Eternal Darkness]** The final reveal (a god presented as a victim orchestrated the whole sequence) works because the evidence was in plain sight and it recontextualises the *reader's* role. Tone: cosmic horror that is never cruel to the reader; the sanity effects are jokes shared with the player.

**The Three-Body Problem trilogy.** Linkage by baton (each protagonist inherits a problem the last created), by hibernation (vast time felt as named eras), and by **embedded texts** (a game, plans, fairy tales that turn out to be the story). The aliens are withheld almost totally and made imposing by *arithmetic* (a fleet 400 years away; a countdown in a scientist's eye) and by having already won the future before arriving. Ye Wenjie is the villain with a reason: her past is told sympathetically, so her decision is understood before it is judged. **[spoiler: Three-Body]** The "dark forest" pays off because a test broadcast the reader forgot is answered years later, converting a theory into a threat and a weapon in one beat; and at least one encoded message in the fairy tales is *never* fully decoded. Tone: an indifferent universe, a tender narrative voice.

**Arrival / "Story of Your Life".** The form is the content: the reader learns to read the narrative the way the linguist learns the language. The aliens are explained by nothing except *their writing*; the script embodies their cosmology. **[spoiler: Arrival]** The reveal changes only the *tense* of scenes already seen. One glyph ("weapon"/"tool"/"gift") is contested, and the whole plot turns on it.

**Mass Effect.** The galaxy is layered archaeology: every species uses relics they don't understand, and every species' history is the same story at a different point on the wheel. The antagonist is introduced as *a ship*, speaks once (the series' best scene), and its motive is most frightening when almost comprehensible and weakest when spelled out. **[spoiler: Mass Effect]** The capital everyone built their government inside is the enemy's device, revealed by a dying fragment in a dead language: the map itself was the plot.

**Ocarina of Time.** The Temple of Time is hub and hinge, tied to every temple through the Sages, who are the child's friends. Repetition with change: every place revisited seven years later, ruined or altered; the world's damage *is* the story. Ganondorf is introduced through a window, then withheld and shown through effects; his imposing quality is spatial. Songs are a language taught by a person in a place, each doing one thing in the world. **[spoiler: OoT]** The seven-year skip works because the player was told for hours "you are too young" and the reward is the loss of the whole world he knew. Tone: dread confined to specific spaces (the Well, the Shadow Temple); the companion voice patient and kind.

**Stargate.** The gate network is a language (seven-symbol addresses); each mythological layer explains the one above (Earth's gods were parasites using stolen technology; the technology belonged to a species that left the physical plane). The "aliens as gods" trope works because the mask slips *early*: the horror is small things pretending to be great. The dialling sequence is epic because it is *legible*: fixed steps with understood meaning. The decipherer is the hero, and **[spoiler: Stargate]** the film pivots on a single missing glyph.

**Star Wars.** Vader is 90% menace and 10% reason, delivered late; **[spoiler: Empire]** the reveal is a re-reading ("from a certain point of view"), not a contradiction, and it changes the hero's goal. The Force is mysticism placed *next to* technology and made to feel older; explaining its mechanism (midichlorians) removed the awe.

### The common core
1. **A reading frame**: a document inside the fiction that the player reads as the characters did; literacy is progress.
2. **Linked lives across time, with the reader as the only continuity**, which makes loss bearable: a character can lose and the story still advances.
3. **Places revisited across ages.** "Everything is related" is spatial before it is narrative.
4. **The antagonist is built from withholding, then given a late, partial, comprehensible reason.** Never an excuse; always a reason.
5. **The power system is the cosmology**: the grammar of the power teaches the lore.
6. **Ancient technology as magic, with the dread that it belongs to someone** who may return.
7. **The best revelations are re-readings**: something already seen changes tense, owner or intention.
8. **Effect first, explanation later.**
9. **A fixed ritual interface**, epic because legible.
10. **The voice is kind even when the world is not.**

### Clichés to avoid (and what the originals did instead)
- *"Ancient evil awakens"* → the evil was already running.
- *The chosen one* → the role is inherited and accidental, then earned.
- *Aliens as gods, lazily* → show the seam early.
- *The lore-dump antagonist* → one speech, late, no mechanism.
- *Explaining the power* → explain meaning, never mechanism.
- *Grim as a style* → confine dread to rooms; contrast makes darkness land.
- *Time jumps as spectacle* → every jump shows a cost to something the player cared about.
- *Anthology without a spine* → one family, object or place must recur.
- *Undecodable-for-its-own-sake script* → the grammar mirrors a worldview.
- *Killing the reader's stand-in to prove seriousness* → the continuity figure always ends with more knowledge than anyone in the story.

## Part 4 — Telling a story in fragments and archives

**The gap between writer and reader** is where found-document stories live: *Dracula* (separate witnesses who never quite overlap; the reader assembles the monster first), *Pale Fire* (a later hand annotating an earlier one is a character), *World War Z* (each voice a trade, a place, one object, one thing they still can't explain: the closest model for 40–80 word voices), *Cloud Atlas* (each life is also an artefact inside the next; one recurring mark is enough to make readers hunt for links), *A Canticle for Leibowitz* (relics preserved and reverently misread across centuries), *Riddley Walker* (a degraded language *is* the story), Chiang's "Exhalation" (the tenderest thing a fragment can do is address its finder without demanding anything), *Roadside Picnic* (some remnants were never meant for us and stay opaque), *Blindsight* (a record can be fluent and have no one behind it). Warning from *House of Leaves*: the "cursed archive" puts the reader in peril for reading; we want the opposite.

**Games:** Dark Souls item descriptions (every item is evidence, but obscurity to a fault: never let one missable fragment be load-bearing); Outer Wilds (conversations, not monologues; a graph of who knew what); *What Remains of Edith Finch* (a different *form* for each life is the strongest way to make ages distinct); *Obra Dinn* (a ledger where inferred facts lock in); *Immortality* (a second read of the same fragment in another light reveals a hidden layer without adding words); *Pentiment* (history contested by later hands; the final act is about which account survives, a kinder ending than "the truth kills"); *Pathologic* (deliberately punishing, makes the player responsible for deaths: avoid).

**Micro-fiction craft.** A 40–80 word fragment must do four jobs: who (a voice with a trade or station), where/when (one concrete sensory anchor), one drawable object, and a hook that is an open *why*, not a "to be continued". Draft at 200 words, cut to 60. Objects outlast people, so across ages **objects are the rhymes**. A **cliffhanger** says "something bad happens unless you return" (pressure); an **open question** says "there is more to know" (pull). For a player in low mood, use only pull.

**Linked lives.** *Cloud Atlas* (six is enough to feel vast; more than eight is a catalogue), *Eternal Darkness* (twelve was too many; voices blurred), *Hyperion* (genre per life, one place they converge on), *The Red Violin* (one object through many hands; its condition carries the history). Working answer for ~125 fragments a year: **5–7 major lives** with 15–25 fragments each, plus a chorus of 20–40 single-fragment witnesses. Convergence without gimmick: seed the link in month one as a plain uncommented detail; let two lives touch by month four; let a later life visibly misread an earlier one; make the final convergence a *recognition*, then add one more thing the player didn't expect.

**Kindness under a dark surface.** Dark works that leave awe rather than despair share: scale as consolation; beauty on the page; *a record survives* (the act of reading is the hope, and the player is that hope by existing); one line of care between two people in a doomed age; an ending that is accepted, not imposed. Moves to avoid for a reader in low mood: cruelty rewarded, cosmic indifference stated as a moral, humour absent for long stretches. Patterns for "the world moves for you, never against you": **all writers are already finished**; **progress is excavation, not intervention** (verbs: found, read, learned, understood; never saved); absence is neutral (dust, not decay; no "while you were gone"); any address to the finder is gratitude, never reproach.

**Voice for an ancient script's speakers.** Foreignness comes from structure and reference, not thee/thou: calques ("she took the long silence"), different measures (time by kiln-firings or "since the third sealing"), fixed opening formulae per age (the cheapest, strongest era marker), honorifics that encode the society, metaphor drawn only from that age's material world. Tolkien's frame: render the oldest age in the plainest concrete English; abstraction rises with recency. Dangers: fantasy-speak (capitalised Abstract Nouns, "the Ancients", portentous ellipses) and generic AI prose (triads, "a testament to", "tapestry", "whispers of", every fragment ending on a wistful cadence). Test: **if a fragment has no noun you could draw, it is not finished.**

## Concrete lessons for this game (binding)

**Structure and truth**
1. **Write the truth first, then the leak schedule.** Every fragment is written in full against the sealed truth, then annotated with what is legible at each sign-coverage band; every partial reading is true-by-omission, never false. The Site never lies; only its writers do.
2. **The ending is latent in the premise.** Choose a premise whose only possible ending is also a surprise.
3. **Count the question ladder and cap it** (five to seven rungs; top rung = the ending). Any later question attaches to an existing rung; none adds one.
4. **Make the promise in the first three fragments and restate it monthly**: many lives, one place, one converging event.
5. **Budget 8–10 model-changing reveals a year (every 5–7 weeks), a confirmation every 1–2 weeks, a noticeable detail in nearly every fragment**, and put the big ones on the calendar before writing anything else. Never three consecutive fragments without a new named fact.
6. **Plan the mid-point (month 5–7) as a single sign or word** that retroactively changes a large share of already-read fragments. It is the design's biggest asset; don't spend it early or by accident.
7. **Trap doors in the lives, not the truth.** Any single life can be cut or shortened if the year runs long or short; the truth and the ladder are fixed, the roster is flexible.

**Fair play and re-reading**
8. **Every double-meaning clue needs a sufficient wrong explanation**, written down. A clue that only reads as a clue is a spoiler.
9. **Let old fragments do the point-back.** When a reveal lands, surface the one or two earlier fragments that now read differently, so fairness is shown, not asserted.
10. **Give every fragment a decodable age-marker** (a formula, a unit, a form). Being wrong about an ordering must be a planted misdirection, never an accident.
11. **If anyone curated the archive, they are a suspect.** Decide who left, hid or assembled the records and plant them through what is *missing* or *out of place*.
12. **Never one fragment load-bearing.** Each structural fact exists in at least two fragments by different witnesses, phrased differently.

**The script**
13. **Sign inventory of roughly 48–60**: ~30–35 content signs, 8–10 function signs (plural, negation, possession, past, "made", question), 4–6 determinatives (person-name ring, place bar, device, age), a small number system. Signs share visual elements so later signs are half-guessable.
14. **Words are 2–4 signs the player already holds**, meaning more than their parts. With signs at 1–2 a week, about one word a month follows naturally.
15. **Use the name-ring (cartouche) from fragment one.** Names are the universal foothold and the spine of "linked lives": Dan should recognise a recurring name before he can read a sign.
16. **The first word by week 2–3 is assembled, not handed**: two signs through a picture-with-caption bilingual (a carved diagram of a gate with the gate-sign beside it, then that sign on a real gate), a third as a partial sign on a big day, and the gate answering as the confirmation.
17. **Guess from three or four candidates, never free text; confirm later, in batches**, never "wrong" at guess time. A rejected guess is a small narrative beat.
18. **Function signs are the best re-reading tools.** Plant negation and past unrecognised (rendered blank) in several early fragments, then learn them around month 2–3 so all those fragments turn at once.
19. **Plan 6–10 re-reading pivots**, each keyed to one sign, across the year. On the visit after the sign is learned, show the old fragment re-rendering with the changed span highlighted.
20. **Leave one sign whose meaning is contested** for months; the mistranslation is a plot beat.
21. **Make word-formation a staged ritual** with fixed steps (place sign, place sign, the word locks, something happens). Legible, repeatable, epic.
22. **Design the grammar to encode the cosmology** (as the rune alignment does): a sign is a concept, and combining signs is combining meanings. Explain meaning, never mechanism.
23. **Fix the end first; write the dictionary backwards from it.** List every sign the final records need; those are the signs that arrive earliest and recur most. The last thing he reads is composed entirely of signs he has held for months.
24. **Decorative script everywhere, learnable core in the records**, plus a small optional second layer for the day he wants to look closer. Never gated, timed or necessary.

**The lives and the antagonist**
25. **Five to seven major lives, ~18 fragments each, plus a chorus of single witnesses.** Fewer feels like a family drama; more can't be told apart at one fragment a day.
26. **One spine**: a lineage, an object or the place itself recurring through every age. Reuse rooms across ages: Dan should find, in a later record, the mark an earlier life left on a wall he has already stood by.
27. **Form per life** (tally, letter, teaching, inventory, confession) and **four voice rules per age**: one fixed opening formula and one unit of time; one forbidden word; metaphor only from that age's material world; the oldest age in the plainest English.
28. **Conversations, not monologues**: at least a third of fragments have two voices disagreeing on the record.
29. **Choose 8–12 recurring objects at the outset**; each passes through at least three hands across ages.
30. **Reveal the antagonist by staircase**: effect (month 1), presence (month 2), face (mid-point), reason (last third), nature (end). Plant the nature hardest.
31. **Plant the antagonist's depth in the records, not in a speech**: when his reason arrives it should re-read something the player has already read and liked.
32. **Make the antagonist right about something.** One who is only wrong is a monster; one who is partly right is a rival thesis, and the ending has to answer him.
33. **Consider a human accomplice with a comprehensible grief** among the lives: the most dangerous record-writer is one Dan half-agrees with.

**Tone and the player**
34. **All writers are finished; progress is excavation.** No character is contemporaneous with Dan; nothing waits; nothing decays in his absence; no text ever references his lateness. Reward verbs: found, read, learned, understood.
35. **Open questions, never cliffhangers.** The pause between fragments costs nothing in the fiction.
36. **Confine dread to rooms.** Designate the places and records where the tone is fully dark; keep the rest melancholy and grand.
37. **Beauty budget**: every dark fragment is paired within its week with one of care, humour or wonder.
38. **Separate the world's voice from the game's voice.** Records may be cruel, mad or despairing; the voice that addresses Dan is warm and plain. An ambiguous victory is allowed; a cruel one to the player is not.
39. **Fragment template (40–80 words)**: an age formula in the first line; one witness identified by trade or station; one drawable object; one concrete measure in that age's counting; one plain fact about the Site; one open question the writer couldn't answer; no address to the finder except gratitude. Each fragment carries exactly one beat (a new name, a sign in context, a contradiction, or a confirmation), and its last line is the one Dan should still remember tomorrow.
40. **The prose test before any fragment ships**: one drawable noun; no capitalised abstraction; no triad; no "whispers"; no wistful final cadence more than once a week.
41. **Budget the ending as generously as the beginning.** "Once I finish the story I stop," so the last re-reading must be the best one.
